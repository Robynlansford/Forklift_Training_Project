MODULE 0: BASIC MACHINE CONTROLS
=================================

STEP 1 — PRE-OPERATION INSPECTION (Do this EVERY time)
-------------------------------------------------------
Before you start the machine, walk around it and check:

   1. Tires — no cracks, properly inflated
   2. Forks or boom — no cracks, no bent tips
   3. Under the machine — no fluid leaks on the ground
   4. Seat belt — buckles and holds
   5. Mirrors — clean and adjusted
   6. Horn — press it, confirm it works
   7. Lights — all working
   8. Parking brake — set it, confirm machine does not roll

If ANY of these fail — DO NOT operate the machine.
Report it immediately to your supervisor.

STEP 2 — GETTING INTO THE MACHINE
----------------------------------
   1. Always approach from the LEFT side
   2. Use the steps and handrails — never jump up or down
   3. Sit fully back in the seat
   4. Adjust so you can reach all pedals comfortably
   5. Buckle your seat belt EVERY single time
   6. Confirm the machine is in NEUTRAL
   7. Engage the parking brake before starting

STEP 3 — STARTING THE MACHINE
------------------------------
   1. Key in, turn to start
   2. Let the engine warm up 30-60 seconds before moving
   3. Test the horn — one quick tap
   4. Test the brakes — press gently, confirm response
   5. Test the hydraulics — raise forks slightly, lower back down

STEP 4 — MAIN CONTROLS (Sit-Down Forklift)
-------------------------------------------
   Steering Wheel   — turns left/right like a car
   Accelerator      — right foot, gentle pressure
   Brake Pedal      — right foot, slows and stops
   Parking Brake    — lever or button, use when parked
   Mast Lift Lever  — pull back = forks go UP
                      push forward = forks go DOWN
   Mast Tilt Lever  — pull back = forks tilt back (safer)
                      push forward = forks tilt forward
   Side-Shift       — moves both forks left or right together
   Horn             — button on steering wheel

STEP 5 — TELEHANDLER-SPECIFIC CONTROLS
---------------------------------------
   Boom Lift        — raises/lowers the entire boom arm
   Boom Extend      — makes boom longer (reaching high trusses)
   Boom Retract     — shortens the boom back in
   Boom Tilt        — adjusts fork angle at end of boom
   Outriggers       — extend from sides for stability on tall lifts
   Steering Modes   — switch between 2-wheel, 4-wheel, and crab

STEP 6 — PICKING UP A LOAD
---------------------------
   1. Approach straight on — never at an angle
   2. Stop about 1 foot away from the load
   3. Lower forks to the correct entry height
   4. Level the forks (not tilted forward or back yet)
   5. Drive forward SLOWLY until forks are fully under the load
   6. Lift slightly — just enough to clear the ground
   7. Tilt forks back a little for stability
   8. Reverse slowly away from the rack or truck

STEP 7 — TRAVELING WITH A LOAD
--------------------------------
   - Keep the load LOW (4 to 8 inches off the ground)
   - Tilt forks slightly BACK
   - Drive slowly
   - Always look in the direction you are traveling
   - Honk before entering intersections or blind corners

STEP 8 — PLACING A LOAD
------------------------
   1. Approach straight on
   2. Raise to the correct placement height
   3. Drive forward slowly into position
   4. Lower the load gently until it is stable
   5. Tilt forks slightly forward to release
   6. Back away slowly — confirm load is stable before leaving

STEP 9 — PARKING AND SHUTDOWN
------------------------------
   1. Lower forks completely to the ground
   2. Tilt forks slightly forward so they rest flat
   3. Shift to NEUTRAL
   4. Set the parking brake
   5. Turn off the engine
   6. Remove the key

MODULE 1: TRUCK PACK & FORK POCKET LOGISTICS
=============================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
In touring production, every inch inside a 53-foot truck counts.
One bad pack means the doors won't close, gear gets damaged,
or the whole load shifts on the highway.

STEP 1 — THE CASTER-SPIN ALIGNMENT (Sideways casters)
------------------------------------------------------
Problem: You push a flight case forward and the swivel casters
turn sideways, stealing 2-3 inches of pack width.

How to fix it:
   1. Push the case forward until you feel the casters resist
   2. Back off ONE inch — just one inch
   3. The casters will swing back to trail straight
   4. Push forward again — the case will now seat flush
   5. Never force it. Brute force buckles caster mounts.

STEP 2 — BREAKING FRICTION ON A PACKED CASE
---------------------------------------------
Problem: A case is wedged so tightly that pulling it forward
drags the neighboring case with it.

How to fix it:
   1. Do NOT pull forward yet
   2. Use the SIDE-SHIFT function first
   3. Shift left and right slightly to break the friction grip
   4. Once the case moves laterally, THEN pull it forward
   5. This protects the neighboring cart from lifting or tipping

STEP 3 — THE FLUSH-FORK RULE
------------------------------
Problem: Your forks are 48 inches long but the cart is only
36 inches deep. The fork tips stick out the back — invisible
to anyone standing behind the cart.

How to handle it:
   1. Do not push the forks all the way through
   2. Keep the extra fork length visible ON YOUR SIDE
   3. The tips stay in front of the cart, where you can see them
   4. This prevents stabbing anyone standing behind the load
   5. Drill this habit — it takes one second and saves lives

MODULE 2: FORK SLOT ANATOMY & PRECISION ALIGNMENT
==================================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
Concert equipment is custom-built and expensive. Missing the
fork pocket by 1 inch means your forks punch through the
cosmetic aluminum skin and destroy what's inside.

STEP 1 — SKIN VS. SLOT (Know what you're looking at)
------------------------------------------------------
   COSMETIC SKIN  = thin aluminum or fiberglass outer panels
                    These are NOT for lifting. Do not touch.

   FORK POCKETS   = reinforced steel guide sleeves built into
                    the internal frame. These are your entry points.

   If you can't see the pockets clearly — STOP and look harder.
   Do not guess. Do not feel your way in while moving.

STEP 2 — VERTICAL RIB SYMMETRY (Finding slots in the dark)
------------------------------------------------------------
Inside a dark truck, floor-level slots are blocked by your mast.

How to find them:
   1. Look at the TOP of the cart — not the bottom
   2. Find the top structural columns, welds, or frame handles
   3. Align your mast with the TOP center of the cart
   4. The floor slots are directly below that center point
   5. If the top looks centered, the forks are centered below

STEP 3 — THE LEVEL-FORK SHADOW TRICK
--------------------------------------
How to confirm your forks are level before entering a slot:

   1. Turn on your headlights
   2. Look at the shadow your forks cast on the face of the cart
   3. Both shadows should be EQUAL WIDTH and PARALLEL
   4. If one shadow is thicker — your forks are tilted
   5. Adjust tilt until both shadows are even
   6. Now enter the slot — you will not bind the steel guides

STEP 4 — NEVER DO A ONE-SIDED LIFT ON TOP-HEAVY LOADS
-------------------------------------------------------
   - Top-heavy carts (motors or heavy gear at the top) will TIP
     the instant you try to lift with only one fork engaged
   - If only one pocket is accessible — STOP
   - Get a supervisor. Re-position the cart. Find another way.
   - One-sided lifts on top-heavy loads are a hard refusal.

MODULE 3: GROUND CREW CHOREOGRAPHY
=====================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
The forklift is the center of a fast-moving human assembly line.
The operator controls the pace of the entire operation.
One wrong move crushes someone or destroys gear.

STEP 1 — THE CLEAR-AND-RELEASE PAUSE (Unloading from a truck)
--------------------------------------------------------------
The danger: Stagehands rush in to grab gear the moment they
see it coming. They will reach in while you are still moving.

The rule — before ANYONE touches the load:
   1. Bring the machine to a COMPLETE STOP
   2. Shift to NEUTRAL
   3. Lower the load until casters are fully on the dock floor
   4. Wait for the load to stop moving
   5. Confirm all hands are clear of your chassis
   6. Only THEN say "All yours" and release the load

Do not rush this pause. It takes 5 seconds and prevents crushes.

STEP 2 — THE 3-FOOT REAR-SWING HALO ZONE
------------------------------------------
Remember: Counterbalance forklifts steer from the REAR.
When you turn the wheel, the BACK of the machine swings out —
sometimes 3-4 feet — in the opposite direction of your turn.

Before ANY sharp turn:
   1. One short horn tap (half a second)
   2. Make eye contact with anyone nearby
   3. Confirm the 3-foot zone behind and beside you is clear
   4. THEN begin your turn
   5. If anyone steps into that zone mid-turn — STOP immediately

STEP 3 — PUSHER'S BLINDNESS YIELD RULE
----------------------------------------
When 4 stagehands push an 8-foot tall cart, the crew in the
back CANNOT see anything ahead of them. They have massive
rolling momentum and zero forward visibility.

The rule is simple:
   - The forklift ALWAYS yields to active pushing crews
   - You can see them. They cannot see you.
   - Stop, wait for them to clear, then proceed.
   - Never assume they will stop or swerve for you.

STEP 4 — THE TWO-TONE HORN PROTOCOL
-------------------------------------
Too much honking = crews stop listening. Use the horn precisely:

   SHORT TAP (half second)
   = "Heads up, I'm changing direction or moving nearby"
   = Courtesy only. Not urgent.

   LONG BLAST (continuous)
   = Immediate life-safety danger
   = Everyone stops, looks, gets clear
   = Use ONLY for real emergencies

Never honk constantly. It trains people to ignore the horn.

MODULE 4: HEAVY PHYSICS & VENUE DYNAMICS
==========================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
Concert gear doesn't behave like warehouse pallets. Wind, flex,
cable bridges, and temporary floors create hazards that most
operators have never trained for.

STEP 1 — THE SAIL EFFECT (Wind and LED walls outdoors)
-------------------------------------------------------
A 20-foot LED wall or fabric sail creates massive wind drag.
Even a 15 mph breeze can try to tip your machine over.

Before any outdoor high lift:
   1. Check the wind speed
   2. If wind is at or above 15 mph — STOP. Do not lift.
   3. If wind is below 15 mph, apply derating:
      Assume your safe capacity is 20-30% LOWER than the chart
   4. Deploy ALL outriggers / stabilizers before extending boom
   5. Keep your movements slow and smooth — no quick motions

STEP 2 — TRUSS BUNDLE DEFLECTION (Long aluminum truss)
-------------------------------------------------------
40-foot truss bundles are flexible. Lift them wrong and they
bow, flex, and can slide off your forks or warp permanently.

How to lift long truss correctly:
   1. Spread your forks to MAXIMUM width
   2. Position forks near the OUTER THIRDS of the bundle
      (not in the center)
   3. Lift slowly and smoothly — no jerking
   4. Drive slowly — bouncing causes the bundle to flex and slide
   5. Never center-lift a long truss bundle

STEP 3 — THE 45-DEGREE CABLE BRIDGE CROSSING
----------------------------------------------
Stadium floors have thick yellow cable ramps protecting
high-voltage power lines. Crossing them straight on with
a heavy machine can crush the cables underneath.

How to cross cable ramps:
   1. Approach the cable ramp at a 45-DEGREE ANGLE
   2. This means one tire crosses at a time — not both at once
   3. Slow down to a crawl
   4. Let the machine roll over the ramp gradually
   5. Never cross straight-on at speed

STEP 4 — STAGE DECK POINT-LOADING
------------------------------------
Temporary stage decks have weight limits per square foot.
A 12,000 lb forklift concentrates all that weight on 4 small
tire contact patches.

Before driving onto any temporary stage deck:
   1. Ask the production manager for the deck's load rating
      (example: 150 lbs per square foot)
   2. Do NOT drive on if you're unsure of the rating
   3. Drive slowly — no hard stops or sharp turns
   4. Spread the load by keeping the machine moving
      (stationary = concentrated point load)

MODULE 5: NIGHT LOAD-IN CHAOS
================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
At 2:00 AM after a long shift, your eyes and brain are not
working at full capacity. The venue adds strobes, haze, and
darkness. Routine moves become dangerous.

STEP 1 — FLASH BLINDNESS PROTOCOL
-----------------------------------
Lighting crews test strobes without warning. When a bright
flash hits you, your night vision is wiped out for seconds.

What to do:
   1. STOP the machine IMMEDIATELY — velocity drops to zero
   2. Do not try to keep moving slowly — you are blind
   3. Do not lean out the cage to see better
   4. Do not trust muscle memory to finish the move
   5. Hold position. Wait for vision to return.
   6. Only then continue.

Zero velocity when vision is compromised. Every time.

STEP 2 — TIRE SCRUBBING ON SENSITIVE FLOORS
---------------------------------------------
On NBA hardwood courts or event venue floors covered with
temporary plywood, a spinning pivot turn will:
   - Burn through the plywood
   - Gouge and scar the hardwood underneath
   - Cost the venue thousands of dollars — billed to the tour

How to turn without scrubbing:
   1. Keep the machine ROLLING slightly during all turns
   2. Never execute a full stationary pivot (spinning in place)
   3. Use slow, arcing turns — like a car making a U-turn
   4. If space is too tight for an arc — back and fill slowly
      in small increments rather than spinning

STEP 3 — ICE RINK INSULATION TRAP
------------------------------------
Many arenas have temporary insulated decking over ice rinks.
Parking a heavy machine in one spot causes the panels to:
   - Compress under the concentrated weight
   - "Cold-sweat" where the insulation meets the ice below
   - Create a subtle sag that tilts your machine

The rule:
   1. Never park heavy loads long-term over arena ice decking
   2. Keep the machine or load moving, or move it to a solid
      perimeter edge where structural support exists
   3. If you must hold position, keep the load elevated —
      not sitting on the deck

MODULE 6: RIGGING COORDINATION & UP-LOOK PROTOCOL
===================================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
High-riggers work 40-80 feet above the floor. Tools, shackles,
and hardware can fall. A 2 lb shackle falling 60 feet hits
with the same force as a 120 lb weight dropped 1 foot.
The airspace above you is a deadly work zone.

STEP 1 — THE UP-LOOK PROTOCOL (Required at every rigging zone)
---------------------------------------------------------------
Before entering ANY area where overhead rigging is active:

   1. STOP the machine at the edge of the zone
   2. Look UP — scan the entire overhead area slowly
   3. Look for dangling hardware, loose motors, active riggers
   4. Confirm the drop zone is clear
   5. Radio or verbally confirm with the rigging lead that
      it is safe to proceed
   6. Only after confirmation — enter the zone

This takes 15 seconds. It is mandatory. Every single time.

STEP 2 — MANAGING GROUND AND OVERHEAD HAZARDS SIMULTANEOUSLY
--------------------------------------------------------------
Sometimes ground crew is pushing a load in your path AT THE
SAME TIME that riggers are working overhead.

How to handle both at once:
   1. Call "STOP" — halt all ground movement first
   2. Wait for the "STOP" echo from ground crew
   3. Confirm the 3-foot halo zone is clear around your machine
   4. Now do your Up-Look — scan above you
   5. Only after BOTH ground and overhead are confirmed clear
      do you proceed
   6. Never manage only one hazard at a time

STEP 3 — SLING LIFTS ON BARE FORKS
-------------------------------------
Sometimes slings or chains are choked around the bare forks
to lift audio arrays or structures.

What changes compared to a normal pallet lift:
   - The load swings freely — it is not rigid
   - Any movement causes a pendulum effect
   - This shifts your machine's center of gravity constantly
   - Slow down ALL movements by at least 50%
   - No sudden starts, stops, or direction changes
   - Have a qualified rigger control the load with a tag line

MODULE 7: FATIGUE, PRESSURE & STOP-WORK AUTHORITY
===================================================

WHY THIS MATTERS
----------------
The biggest risk on a 2:00 AM load-out is not the machine.
It is the exhausted operator who pushes past their limit.
Fatigue causes delayed reactions, tunnel vision, and microsleep.
These kill people and end tours.

STEP 1 — KNOW YOUR FATIGUE SIGNS
----------------------------------
Watch for these in yourself:

   - Eyes that feel heavy or keep losing focus
   - Moments where you "wake up" and realize you zoned out
   - Slowed reaction time — things feel like slow motion
   - Forgetting your last few moves
   - Irritability or difficulty making simple decisions

If ANY of these happen during an operation — stop immediately.

STEP 2 — HOW TO INVOKE STOP-WORK AUTHORITY
--------------------------------------------
Stop-Work Authority means ANY person on the production crew
has the right — and the obligation — to halt an unsafe operation.
As the operator, you have full authority. Use it.

When you feel unsafe or recognize a hazard:

   1. Say "STOP" — loudly and clearly
   2. Freeze the machine — all hydraulics and motion halt
   3. State your reason calmly:
      "I am fatigued and need relief before continuing."
      "This load is compromised and is not safe to travel."
      "The rigging zone is not clear."
   4. Do not proceed until the issue is resolved

STEP 3 — HOW TO HANDLE PRODUCTION PRESSURE
--------------------------------------------
Scenario: The Tour Manager is screaming that the truck leaves
in 20 minutes and you need to push through.

What NOT to do:
   - Do not fold and say "okay, I'll just be careful"
   - Do not argue, get emotional, or raise your voice
   - Do not threaten to quit or escalate personally

What TO do — use professional, factual language:
   - "The load is structurally compromised. It cannot travel."
   - "I am past safe operating limits. I need relief."
   - "The rigging zone is not cleared. We cannot proceed."

These phrases are objective and unarguable. They remove emotion.
A professional production manager will respect them.
An accident cancels the tour. A 15-minute delay does not.

STEP 4 — THE HERO OPERATOR TRAP
---------------------------------
"Hero Operator" = someone who pushes through exhaustion
and pressure to "save the show."

Why this backfires:
   - Reaction time at 14+ hours is severely degraded
   - Tunnel vision causes missed hazards in the periphery
   - Microsleep can cause a dropped load or crush injury
   - One accident does not just hurt one person —
     it shuts down the whole tour, potentially permanently

Being the operator who calls STOP when it counts IS saving
the show. Every time.

CAPSTONE SCENARIO: THE 2:00 AM ARENA LOAD-OUT
===============================================

SITUATION
----------
Time: 2:00 AM
Shift: 14 hours in
Load: 10,000 lb LED wall section (massive sail surface)
Wind: 17 mph gusting
Rigging: High-riggers active 60 ft above center stage
Visibility: Low — haze and dim house lights
Pressure: Tour Manager needs the truck to roll in 45 minutes

Walk through each step correctly:

STEP 1 — APPROACHING THE RIGGING ZONE
--------------------------------------
   WRONG: Drive in and start positioning under the rigging
   RIGHT: Stop at the zone edge. Perform Up-Look. Radio the
          rigging lead. Wait for verbal "clear" before entering.

STEP 2 — WIND HITS 17 MPH WHILE RAISING THE BOOM
--------------------------------------------------
   WRONG: Continue raising because you're "almost there"
   RIGHT: STOP. Call "STOP" on radio. 17 mph exceeds the 15 mph
          threshold. The LED wall will act as a sail. Hold
          position until wind drops or conditions change.

STEP 3 — GROUND CREW RUSHES IN BEFORE YOU SIGNAL
--------------------------------------------------
   WRONG: Lower the load to them while they're reaching in
   RIGHT: Call "STOP." Wait for the echo. Confirm the 3-foot
          halo is clear. Perform a quick Up-Look. Only then
          lower the load for the hand-off.

STEP 4 — YOU FEEL A MICROSLEEP COMING ON
-----------------------------------------
   WRONG: Push through — it's just one more lift
   RIGHT: Stop the machine. Radio: "Operator calling STOP —
          requesting relief." Step down from the machine.

PASSING THIS CAPSTONE REQUIRES ALL FOUR CORRECT.
One shortcut. One skipped step. One "just this once."
That is how careers end and people get hurt.
